I Never Said It Right Once, and It Wrote 3,600 Lines: Five Corrections Behind One Vibe Video

Created2026-11-16|Updated2026-11-16|Personal Website
|Word Count:2.9k|Reading Time:18mins|Post Views:

That 61-second film on the site (/en/video/) — I didn’t write a single line of its code.

But every frame of it looks the way it does because I yelled at it, one sentence at a time.

This isn’t a tutorial on installing Remotion, and it won’t teach you what interpolate does — the docs have that. This is about something else: how a person who doesn’t really code used five sentences to drag a film that was “technically flawless but made me want to close the tab” into what it is now.

The real process wasn’t elegant. Out of everything I said, only the first sentence was “here’s what I want.” The other four were all “no.”

Five exchanges. The middle column is what it handed back; the last column is my verdict.

1. The first time I opened my mouth, I said one sentence

Word for word:

Make a promo video for my blog, in this style

Followed by a link.

I thought that was enough. A goal (promote my blog), a style (that one video I picked), and the rest should be its problem.

And it didn’t keep me waiting. Here’s what came back:

  • 12 scenes, 50.7 seconds
  • Chinese voiceover narration, reading my copy line by line
  • 3,600 lines of tsx, npx tsc --noEmit passing on the first try
  • Palette, typography, transitions, progress bar — all there

Technically, beyond reproach. I was honestly a little stunned — this is three days of an editor’s work.

But I watched it start to finish and never once wanted to pause, or watch a second time.

That’s the problem. It had read “promo video” as a delivery checklist: an intro, a body, an outro, voiceover, music, captions. Every line item ticked. What I actually wanted was something else, and that something wasn’t on the checklist — because I never said it out loud.

That was my first mistake, and the root of everything that followed:

Say only what you want, and you get “a passing sample,” not “the one you had in mind.”

The model can’t see the film in your head. It sees the handful of words you gave it and drifts toward the average. The average is always correct, and always forgettable.

2. First correction: I gave it a reference, but I only said “don’t”

Second sentence:

No voiceover. Put in proper music instead — reference the example I gave you.

I got one thing right in that sentence and one thing wrong.

Right: I swapped “sounds good” for something it could compare against — the reference film I’d picked. Before that, “good music” was a fuzzy feeling in my head that couldn’t be executed. With a reference film, it could at least go measure that film’s BPM, spectrum, and energy curve. That’s the move from adjective to reference point, and I’d use it over and over.

Wrong: I said “no voiceover” without saying “so what happens to the timeline.”

Here’s what came back: the voiceover component was indeed gone from the timeline, but when I opened public/, the vo/ folder and its dozen mp3s were still sitting there. The music was in — at -20dB. It probably figured background music should be quiet, that being the whole point of “background.”

I listened three times with headphones before I was sure: the voiceover was still there, and the music was too quiet to hear.

A negative has no boundary. Say “not X” and it deletes the most visible X, without wondering what the entire apparatus built to serve X should become.

The timeline had been laid out around the length of spoken sentences. Remove the voice, and those lengths become twelve stretches of inexplicable silence. It didn’t notice, because I didn’t mention it.

3. Second correction: I snapped at it, but I snapped in the right place

Third sentence — I was genuinely annoyed:

??? why is there still voiceover, and there’s still no background music

Rude, but it worked, and it worked for a reason: I gave it two facts it could verify immediately. Voiceover still present (checkable). Music inaudible (measurable).

This time it did well. It didn’t just turn the volume up — it did three things:

  1. Deleted public/vo/, tools/build-audio.mjs, src/words.json so I could confirm the whole voiceover chain was severed;
  2. Re-laid the timeline, recomputing twelve scenes into 61.4 seconds / 1,842 frames;
  3. Produced a number: the correlation between the final audio track and my score.wav was 0.9999.

That 0.9999 was the turning point of the whole collaboration. It converted “I can still hear voiceover” — an argument nobody can win — into something you can judge at a glance.

Since then I’ve changed how I accept work:

When something feels wrong, find a way to turn it into a number. “The sound is too quiet” is an argument. “-11.7 LUFS” is a conclusion.

As an aside: it went deeper on the score than I expected. It first analyzed the reference film (Emergence) and measured 60 BPM, with 20–120Hz at 57.6% and low-end hits spaced 0.75–1.75 seconds apart. Then it synthesized eight layers from scratch in numpy — pad, sub, arpeggio, shimmer, bell, heartbeat pulse, air, impact — tuned each layer’s gain scene by scene, and finally pushed it to -11.7 LUFS with loudnorm.

I still have the spectrum comparison it drew:

Left: four-band energy share, reference film vs. mine. Right: long-term average spectrum. The reference sits at 57.6% in 20–120Hz; mine is 37.6% — because its low end is propped up by a voiceover, and my film has none, so the mids have to carry themselves.

That was the first time I realized: it can do more of this than I assumed. All I had to say was “reference that.” It translated “reference” into BPM, frequency bands, and a gain table — things I genuinely don’t understand.

4. Third correction: “too empty” is a word I made up, and it doesn’t know it

The film had sound now. I watched the picture again. Then I said the fourth sentence:

The content is still too empty. There’s nothing there. Not striking enough.

“Too empty” — those two words point at something very specific in my head. I was looking at the reference film, and I remembered how dense every screen of it was: a chart with axes, twenty-odd data points, hundreds of birds, three red boxes, a one-line conclusion at the bottom, a citation in the corner.

But I said none of that. I said “too empty.”

Its first response was perfectly logical: more text. Bigger text, more of it, flashier entrances. The second cut was emptier than the first — because every pixel on screen was saying the same sentence.

I knew where the problem was: I was using an adjective and expecting it to guess the exact picture in my head.

So I changed the question. I didn’t say “add more stuff.” I told it to count:

Pull frames from the reference film second by second, look at them one screen at a time, and tell me how many independent information elements each screen has. List them.

It counted: axes, data points, annotations, a conclusion, a source — at least five per screen.

My film had one centered line of large type and a field of black.

That was “too empty” in countable form. From that moment, “too empty” stopped being an adjective and became a gap: five minus one.

The next round rewrote eight scenes, and I signed off on them one by one:

  • 02 Who I am: was one big “Data person.” Became a left-hand title plus a bar chart of real post counts across 12 categories (Finance & Investing 5, Distributed Database 4, …), where bar length is the count.
  • 03 Fragments: was three words. Became three real diagrams — a 5-replica log stream (ack 3/5 → COMMIT), a Saga state machine with a red dashed compensation edge, and a genuinely rotating SG90 servo with an angle plot.
  • 07 Scale: was three numbers. Became a 24-post real publishing timeline, one dot per article, the 301-day gap marked with hatching, plus a cumulative step line on top.
  • 08 The gap: was one canyon. Added depth ticks down the wall (0 / 3 years / 7 / 10), four strata lines, 46 falling specks, and “no tutorial / no teacher / no control group” written inside the chasm.
  • 09 The bridge: was one character (“cross”). Became a six-layer knowledge stack with a light climbing from the 2010s, each layer lighting up as it arrives, each tagged with the article it came from.
  • 10 Close: was two lines. Added a 962-day evidence strip — 24 bars by date, height by length, with a red “post 25 · in progress” growing at the right end.

The twelve screens after the rewrite. This is my check sheet: pick any cell at random and ask whether it contains something that only I could have.

After that round, the film was watchable.

An abstract adjective has to be translated into a countable fact. “Striking” isn’t countable. “How many independent elements are on this screen” is. Swap the word for a number and it knows what to do.

5. Fourth correction: the frame was wrong, and editing content couldn’t fix it

I was happy with the film, so I decided to write the whole thing up as a tutorial for the blog.

It wrote one. Fifteen sections, clean structure: why not use editing software, what to install, setting up the project, how to lay out script.json, aligning the timeline to 60 BPM, handling transitions, drawing a screen, palette and type, animation functions, synthesizing the score, frame-pulling for debugging, exporting, every trap I hit, and a closing section.

Technically all correct. The code was real and runnable. Plenty of words, too — six thousand.

I read it twice, then told it:

I’ve read what you wrote. What I want to see isn’t how you made it — I want how I guided the AI to make it. That’s why it’s called vibe knowledge. Understand?

It had written a technical document. I wanted a human record.

This wasn’t a content problem, it was a frame problem. Every paragraph was correct, and there was no “I” in it — none of my bad judgment calls, none of the moments I said the wrong thing, none of the reason I stopped and rewatched a particular frame three times.

If I’d only said “revise it,” “add an example,” “lighten the tone,” it would have polished that wrong frame more and more finely, drifting further from what I wanted with every pass.

When the frame is wrong, “revise it” is poison. It traps you in an increasingly beautiful wrong direction. The only useful move is: stop, state the frame, start over.

So I had it delete 630 lines and rewrite. That’s what you’re reading.

6. Four crude rules I worked out

Five exchanges, four rules. None of them are profound, but I paid for every one with a dead cut.

Rule one: give a reference point, not an adjective

“Good-looking,” “premium,” “textured,” “striking” — these mean something in my head and nothing to it. But “reference this film” or “follow that site’s layout” is something that can be taken apart, measured, and copied.

The reference film I gave it — it extracted 60 BPM, 57.6% sub-bass, five information elements per screen. None of that was mine. It could measure it because I gave it something measurable.

Rule two: when you say “don’t,” also say what to do instead

The danger of “no voiceover” is that it has no terminus. It deleted the voiceover, and with it everything the voiceover had been holding up — the length of the timeline, the rhythm of each scene, the place where attention landed.

What I learned to say instead: not A, make it B, and fill the hole A left with C.

Rule three: acceptance needs a number

“I can still hear voiceover” and “audio correlation 0.9999” are two different species. The first invites “I think it’s gone now.” The second has no room for discussion.

Now I force myself to find the number: what fraction of the spectrum, how many frames total, how many elements on this screen, how many seconds to render. Even when I make the number up, it beats a feeling.

Rule four: if it feels wrong and you can’t say why, ask what you’re comparing it to

Once I stared at scene three for a long time without being able to name what was off. Turned out I was comparing it, in my head, to a specific frame of the reference film — a frame I had never put into a single instruction.

Not being able to name the problem usually means the reference point is still inside your head. Say it out loud and the problem shows itself.

7. Three vetoes I exercised

Some things it couldn’t do, and shouldn’t have. I remember all three vetoes.

Veto one: the crossfade.

Its first transitions were standard fades — old screen fading out, new screen fading in. Correct by every tutorial ever written. But I pulled the frame at 8.2 seconds and the two centered headlines had dissolved into each other, a blurry mess.

I watched it three times before I was sure it wasn’t my eyes. Then I said: stop overlapping. Let the old one leave clean, then bring the new one in.

After the fix there’s a single near-black frame in between. That one frame is what made the whole film breathe.

The fixed transition: the outgoing scene clears completely (7 frames), one black frame, then the incoming scene enters (7 frames). Scale lifts slightly on the way out; blur only appears on exit and entry.

Veto two: those twelve grey lines.

Scene six, “write it down.” It drew twelve grey horizontal lines on the right, each slightly shorter than the last, and called it “rhythm.” I looked for a while and asked: what do these twelve lines each represent?

It couldn’t answer. So they were decoration.

Those twelve lines became nine real “old belief → correction” pairs, each traceable to one of my articles. Struck-through belief on the left, correction on the right.

Veto three: the voiceover.

No explanation needed. I listened three times with headphones before I dared conclude it wasn’t my imagination.

All three vetoes share one thing: they were judgments I made after watching and listening. The AI can’t do that part for me — it can compute a spectrum share, but it doesn’t know that two lines of type at 8.2 seconds look bad.

8. So what exactly did “I” do

After the film was done I sat down and split the work into “mine” and “its.”

Mine:

  • Picked the reference film. That choice set the film’s ceiling — had I picked something garish, no amount of revision would have saved it from being garish.
  • Decided “no voiceover.” That decision set the film’s form, and forced the score to carry the mids alone.
  • Counted “five elements per screen” and turned it into an executable density standard.
  • Stopped at 8.2 seconds and vetoed the crossfade.
  • Saw twelve grey lines and asked what they meant.
  • Listened three times and confirmed the voiceover was still there.
  • Pressed enter before every re-render.
  • And wrote this.

Its:

  • 3,600 lines of tsx across twenty files.
  • Translating “reference it” into 60 BPM, eight layers, a gain table, loudnorm.
  • Turning 24 articles’ dates and lengths into a timeline.
  • Running its own type checks, pulling its own frames, fixing its own errors after every change.
  • When I said delete 630 lines, it deleted them.

I counted: I said maybe twenty effective sentences for this film. It wrote 3,600 lines.

Vibe isn’t “I said less.” It’s “I judged precisely.” I said less because it genuinely can do most of the work. I judged precisely because behind every “no” there was a specific picture in my head.

9. If you want to try this

Five things, in order of importance:

One. Find the one film that is “exactly this.” Not “roughly that feeling” — one specific film, one specific frame. This is worth more than the other four combined.

Two. State your dissatisfaction as a fact, not a mood. “Too empty” will work. “Besides the headline, is there anything else in scene three?” works better.

Three. Accept that you’ll say the wrong thing. Four of my five sentences were “no.” That isn’t failure; that’s the shape of the process.

Four. Make it verify itself. Type checks, frame pulls, spectrum measurements, correlation coefficients — anything that can become a number, make it hand you the number.

Five. When the frame is wrong, start over. Don’t patch. Most expensive rule, biggest time saver.

10. The film is here

61.4 seconds, 1080p, thirty frames per second, no narration, -11.7 LUFS.

/en/video/

If you watch it and think “that’s decent,” then inside it are about twenty sentences I said and three thousand six hundred lines it wrote. That’s the division of labor.

Reference film: Emergence · An Epic of Trial (Bilibili, my favorite). Stack: Remotion + React + TypeScript, score synthesized from scratch in numpy. The whole project is 3,640 lines across twenty tsx files.
